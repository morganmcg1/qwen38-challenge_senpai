A sequence model built from two kinds of layer has to reconcile two different
theories of memory. The first holds that everything worth knowing about the
past can be compressed into a state of fixed size, updated once per position
and never revisited. The second holds that the past should be kept in full,
stored position by position, and consulted again whenever a later position has
a question to ask of it. Neither theory is adequate alone. A fixed state is
cheap to carry but must continually decide what to forget; a complete record
forgets nothing but grows without bound and must be searched. A hybrid
architecture accepts both costs in different places, giving most of its depth
to compressive recurrence and reserving a minority of its layers for the
expensive and exact kind of recall.

When such a model is given a long document to read before it is asked to
produce anything, the document arrives as a stack of embeddings, one row for
each position, and passes upward through the layers. In a recurrent layer the
computation is organised along the sequence rather than across it. The layer
carries a state, conventionally a matrix rather than a vector, and at each
position it forms a few projections of the incoming activation: something
playing the role of a key, something playing the role of a value, and one or
more gates. The gates are the interesting part. One of them decides how much
of the existing state survives into the next position, and because it is
computed from the current input rather than fixed in advance, the layer can
hold a fact almost indefinitely when the input suggests that it should, and
discard it abruptly when the input suggests that it should not. The state is
then updated by accumulating an outer product of the new key and value, scaled
and decayed according to the gates, and the layer's output is read back out of
the state by a query projection. Nothing in this loop refers to any position
except the current one and the summary of everything before it, so the memory
required does not depend on the length of the document, and the arithmetic
grows in direct proportion to it.

A full-attention layer treats the same activation quite differently. Queries,
keys and values are projected as before, but the keys and values are retained.
Each position writes its key and value into a cache indexed by layer and
position, and the query at a given position is compared against every key at
or before it. The scores are normalised across the attended positions and the
output is the correspondingly weighted sum of values. Two properties of this
arrangement dominate its cost. During the first pass over the document the
work grows with the square of the length, because every position consults
every earlier one. And the cache that makes later recall cheap is also the
largest runtime object the model holds, growing linearly in the number of
positions, the number of retained heads and the width of each head. Sharing
one key and value head among several query heads is a concession to that
second property and to very little else.

Extending the document one position at a time inverts the balance. The
recurrent layers do exactly what they did before, one state update apiece,
indifferent to how much text preceded them. The attention layers now perform a
single query against a long cache, which is arithmetically trivial and yet
often the slowest thing in the step, because the cache must be read out of
memory in its entirety while the useful multiplications are few. The step is
limited by traffic rather than by computation, and the same holds for the
projection weights, which must be streamed once per position regardless of how
little is asked of them.

The practical lesson is that the two mechanisms fail in opposite directions,
and the hybrid is arranged to exploit that. Length hurts attention and leaves
recurrence untouched; precision of recall hurts recurrence and comes almost
free to attention. Reading a long context is expensive in arithmetic and cheap
in traffic, while continuing it position by position is cheap in arithmetic and
expensive in traffic. Anyone who reasons about the cost of such a model without
first saying which of the two regimes is meant will reliably reach the wrong
conclusion, and will optimise the half of the system that was never the
constraint.
