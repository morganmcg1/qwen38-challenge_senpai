I left the harbour at half past seven with the tide going out and the whole bay
the colour of wet slate. The coastal road climbs at once out of the town, past
a chapel with a tin roof and a yard of leaning stones, and by the second bend
the roofs below had arranged themselves into the pattern you see on postcards:
grey, wet, tightly packed, with three boats aground on the mud and a fourth
still afloat in the channel. There had been rain in the night. The tarmac was
dark and steaming faintly where the sun found it, and the hedge banks on either
side were full of water moving in small invisible channels, so that the whole
hillside made a continuous thin noise, not loud, but impossible to walk out of.

Autumn had got into the bracken. It was not the clean gold of photographs but a
dozen browns at once, foxed and rusted, with the dead fronds pressed flat where
the wind had lain on them and the new ones still standing in among the old. The
gorse was black and dripping. Where the road ran level, blackthorn had grown
over from the seaward bank and closed above me, and for a hundred yards I
walked in a tunnel of leaves with the sound of the sea somewhere off to the
right and nothing to see but the wet road. Then the hedge broke and the whole
coast opened out at once: four miles of cliff going north in a series of
headlands, each one paler than the last, and beyond the furthest a smudge that
might have been an island or might have been rain.

The weather came in from the west all morning in separate parcels. I could
watch each one approach, a grey column standing on the water with its own
private darkness under it, and I learned to judge the four or five minutes I
had before it reached the road. Twice I sheltered in the lee of a field wall
built of stones set on edge like the teeth of a comb, and both times the squall
went through in less than the time it took to eat an apple, and afterwards the
light was extraordinary, low and yellow and coming in under the cloud so that
every wet blade of grass on the seaward slope carried a separate point of it.
The gulls hung above the cliff edge without moving, facing the wind, adjusting
a wingtip now and then, and a raven went over the road southward, croaking
twice, in no hurry.

At about eleven the road dropped into a village of perhaps thirty houses set
along a stream, all of them turned side-on to the sea as though refusing to
look at it. There was a pump on a triangle of grass, painted green, with the
handle wired up out of use. A shop sold bread, netting, sweets in jars, and a
newspaper two days old. The woman who served me asked where I was walking to
and then told me, with some satisfaction, that the path above the next headland
had gone into the sea in February and that I would have to go round by the lane
and the farm, which was longer and had a bull in it, though the bull was not a
serious bull. Outside, a man was scrubbing lobster pots with a broom and the
water ran off the granite sett into a drain packed solid with sycamore leaves. A
dog watched me from a doorway without troubling to lift its head, and a child on
a bicycle rode twice around the pump for no reason I could discover and then
went indoors.

I went round by the lane. The farm smelled of silage and diesel and there was
no bull, only a heifer that followed me the length of her field with the
mechanical curiosity of cattle. Beyond the buildings the way climbed again
through a stand of sycamore already half bare, the leaves black and pasted to
the stones, and came out on the top into the full weight of the wind. From
there I could see where I had come from, the harbour reduced to a notch in the
land with a white line of surf across its mouth, and where I was going, which
was a long descent to a beach the colour of ash with a river crossing it in
three shallow channels, and behind the beach a wood, and above the wood the
first proper hill of the peninsula with its head in cloud. It was two o'clock
and it would be dark by six, and I remember thinking, standing there with my
collar wet through and my hands cold, that I had no wish at all to be anywhere
else.
