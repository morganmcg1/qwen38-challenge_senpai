The supply boat came out to the rock on the eleventh of October, and Aldous
Wren stood on the landing stage with his hands pushed into his coat while the
crew swung the crates ashore. There were four crates that autumn where there
had always been six. The mate was a young man from the mainland who had not
been out to the light before, and he mentioned, in the flat cheerful way that
people use for other men's misfortunes, that the tender would call again in
March if the weather allowed, and that after March there would be no
particular reason to call at all. Wren said that he understood. The letter had
reached him in August. He had read it twice and then folded it into the tin
where he kept his father's discharge papers and a photograph of a dog that had
died before the war.

The winter began properly in the second week of November, with a sea that came
from the north and did not change direction for nine days. Wren kept to the
routine he had kept for twenty-two years, because the routine was the only
argument he had ever needed. He wound the clockwork at eight and again at two.
He trimmed and filled, wiped the brass rail on the gallery stair, and polished
the inner face of the lens with a cloth he washed every Sunday in rainwater
because the mainland soap left a bloom on the glass. He wrote the weather in
the log in a small upright hand: wind, sea, visibility, the hour the light was
shown and the hour it was extinguished. He never once wrote that he was alone,
since the log was not for that, and in any case the fact was not remarkable
enough to record.

There were small events. A trawler lost her steering off the shoal in December
and lay hove to for a night within half a mile of him, all her deck lights
burning, and he watched her from the gallery until dawn as though keeping
company. A gannet struck the glass in January and left a smear of oil and one
grey feather wedged in the astragal, and he climbed out in a lull to clean it
and found the bird gone from the rock, taken by the sea or by something else.
Twice the wind rose enough to throw water over the gallery rail, ninety feet
above the mark, and each time he felt the tower move, not sway exactly, but
lean and settle like a man shifting his weight. He had never grown used to it
and had never told anybody that he had not.

What he found difficult was not the weather but the reading of the notice
board. There was nothing on the rock that needed a decision from him, and
knowing that a machine would keep the light after him made every careful thing
he did feel like an argument nobody was listening to. The automatic apparatus
had a name and a specification. It would run for a year on a charge and would
be visited by helicopter twice, and it would not polish anything. He caught
himself, one evening in February, standing in the doorway of the lamp room and
holding the cloth and thinking about the smell of hot metal and paraffin, and
he decided that he would leave the brass exactly as he had always kept it, out
of no motive he could name.

In the last fortnight he went through the store and made an inventory that
nobody had asked him for. Two hundred feet of manila, sound. A case of lamp
glasses, one of them cracked across the shoulder. A drum of oil that had been
on the rock longer than he had, and which he would not have trusted in any
weather. He wrote it all out on the back of a spoiled chart in the same small
upright hand, and weighted the sheet with a shackle on the bench beside the
door, where a man would find it if a man ever came.

The tender came on the nineteenth of March. He had been packed for a week. He
put out the light at twenty past six, as the log required, and wrote the hour,
and signed the page, and left the log on the shelf where the next man, who was
not a man, would not read it. The mate had brought a colleague and they took
his boxes down while he waited on the stage. From the water he watched the
tower until the swell put it behind a shoulder of rock, and then he turned and
looked at the mainland, which was low and brown and full of chimneys, and he
thought that it looked like a place where nothing was ever quite dark.
