Breathlessness that has come on over months rather than minutes is among the
commonest complaints in general medicine and among the most frequently
mishandled, because the symptom is single and its causes are many. The history
carries most of the diagnostic weight, and it is worth taking slowly. Ask
first what the patient can no longer do, and insist upon a concrete answer:
the number of stairs, the distance on level ground before stopping, the weight
of shopping that must now be divided into two journeys. A man who says only
that he is short of breath, and who has given up the allotment he worked until
last spring, has told you a great deal more than his own summary suggested.
Establish the tempo as well. A decline over two or three years, punctuated by
winter chest infections, points differently from a decline over six weeks in
someone who was previously well.

Then take the associated symptoms in turn. Waking at night gasping, with
relief on sitting forward, suggests raised pulmonary venous pressure, and so
does ankle swelling that worsens through the evening and is absent on waking.
Wheeze and a productive morning cough point toward the airways, particularly
in a smoker. A dry cough with effort intolerance, unaccompanied by wheeze, in
a patient whose fine crackles do not clear with coughing, should raise the
possibility of interstitial disease. Chest pain, syncope on exertion, or
breathlessness grossly out of proportion to the physical findings deserves
particular respect. Ask about occupation and about hobbies, since the dusts of
a working lifetime and the birds in an outhouse are equally capable of
scarring a lung, and ask about every drug taken, including those the patient
does not think of as drugs.

On examination, look before you listen. Count the respiratory rate over a full
minute while appearing to take the pulse. Note whether the patient can
complete a sentence, whether the accessory muscles are working, whether there
is central cyanosis, and what the oxygen saturation does after a walk along
the corridor, which is often more informative than the value at rest. A raised
jugular venous pressure, a displaced apex, a third heart sound and bibasal
crackles together make a cardiac cause probable; quiet breath sounds, a
hyperinflated chest and a prolonged expiratory phase make an obstructive one
probable. The findings are commonly mixed, and in the elderly two diseases are
more likely than one.

The initial investigation is unglamorous and should be complete. A full blood
count will disclose the anaemia that occasionally accounts for the entire
complaint. Renal function, thyroid function and a natriuretic peptide are each
worth having, the last chiefly for its negative predictive value, since a
normal concentration in an untreated patient makes heart failure unlikely and
redirects the enquiry. An electrocardiogram that is wholly normal argues
against left ventricular systolic impairment. The chest radiograph may show
cardiomegaly, effusion, hyperinflation, reticular shadowing or a mass, and
should be looked at rather than accepted at second hand. Spirometry with
reversibility testing, echocardiography, and, where the plain film or the
crackles suggest it, high resolution computed tomography will settle most of
the remainder. Order what the history has made plausible, and be prepared to
return to the history when the results do not fit it.

Management follows the mechanism, and the temptation to treat the label
instead must be resisted. In fluid overload a loop diuretic titrated against
weight and symptoms gives quick relief, but durable benefit comes from the
disease-modifying agents introduced early and advanced toward their target
doses, with attention to rate, rhythm and the valves. In obstructive airways
disease the choice of inhaled drug matters less than the technique with which
it is delivered, and neither matters as much as stopping smoking and enrolling
the patient in pulmonary rehabilitation, which improves exercise capacity more
reliably than any bronchodilator. Where the disease is interstitial, refer
early. Throughout, treat deconditioning as a real and reversible contributor
rather than a diagnosis of exclusion, ask whether every symptom has been
accounted for, and arrange review, because the commonest error in this
presentation is not the wrong diagnosis but the incomplete one.
