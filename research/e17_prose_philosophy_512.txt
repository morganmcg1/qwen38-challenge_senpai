I want to examine a saying that is repeated so often that it has stopped being
examined, namely that to measure a thing is to change it. Stated loosely, the
saying is either trivially true or plainly false, and the interest lies in the
distinctions that separate those two readings. Let me begin with the version I
take to be trivially true. If I put a cold thermometer into a small cup of
warm water, the water gives up a little of its heat to the glass, and the
number I read is therefore the temperature of the water after my intrusion, not
before it. Nobody is troubled by this. It is a defect of the instrument, and
it can be reduced by making the instrument smaller, or by extrapolating from
several readings, or by choosing a method that touches nothing at all. The
disturbance is real, but it is contingent. It belongs to my clumsiness rather
than to the nature of measuring.

Now consider the version I take to be plainly false, or at least badly stated.
Some hold that because every observation involves an observer, no quantity has
a determinate value until it is looked at, and therefore that measurement is
not a discovery but a kind of authorship. I resist this. When I return to a
room I left an hour ago and find the kettle cold, I do not conclude that the
kettle had no temperature while I was absent and acquired one on my return. I
conclude that it cooled, in accordance with the same rule that governed it
while I watched. The rule, and not my attention, is doing the work. To make the
observer the author of the quantity is to mistake the order of dependence: my
knowledge depends on the kettle, and the kettle is quite indifferent to my
knowledge.

Against this an objection may be raised, and it is the strongest one I know. It
holds that I have chosen a friendly example. Kettles are large and sluggish and
tolerant of being examined. But there are things whose whole existence consists
in a pattern of behaviour, and to observe such a thing is necessarily to enter
the pattern. If I wish to know how a man behaves when unobserved, I cannot
learn it by watching him, and no refinement of my technique will help, because
the difficulty is not in my instrument but in the definition of what I am after.
The disturbance here is not contingent at all. It is constitutive.

I accept the objection, and I think it forces a division rather than a
concession. There are properties a thing has independently of any procedure,
and there are properties that are only defined relative to a procedure. The
mass of a stone belongs to the first sort. The popularity of a policy belongs
to the second, since there is no fact about popularity apart from some way of
asking, and different ways of asking are different questions rather than better
and worse approaches to one question. The saying I began with is true of the
second sort and misleading about the first, and most of the confusion in
ordinary talk comes from applying it indifferently to both.

A second objection presses further. Even where the property is independent, my
choice of what to measure shapes what is subsequently done, and so the
measurement changes the world by way of the actions it licenses. A school that
is graded on one number will reorganise itself around that number, and the
number will consequently become a worse description of the school than it was
on the day it was first taken. To this I say that the effect is genuine and
important, but it is not a fact about measurement. It is a fact about
incentives. The thermometer did not corrupt the school; the decision to pay for
a reading did. Keeping these apart matters practically, because the two have
opposite remedies. Against disturbance one refines the instrument. Against
corruption one changes what depends on the result, and refining the instrument
only makes the corruption more precise.

My conclusion is modest. Measurement changes what it measures whenever the
measuring is part of the thing, and otherwise it changes only what we do next.
The first case calls for honesty about definitions, the second for care about
consequences, and neither is served by the comfortable fatalism of the saying
itself.
