Before the railways, every English town kept its own clock. Noon was a local
fact, established by the sun over a particular parish, and a traveller moving
west from London gained a minute of the morning for every fourteen miles of
road. Bristol ran ten minutes behind the capital, Exeter fourteen, Penzance
something over twenty, and nobody was inconvenienced by the discrepancy,
because nobody could move fast enough for it to matter. A coach that took two
days to reach Devon had no need of a shared second. The mail guards carried
timepieces that were deliberately set to gain a fixed amount each day, and the
guard adjusted his reckoning as he went, so that the letters arrived at an hour
the receiving postmaster could recognise. This was not disorder. It was a
perfectly serviceable arrangement for a country whose fastest information
travelled at the speed of a horse.

The locomotive dissolved the arrangement in about a decade. A train leaving
Paddington at a stated hour and arriving at Bridgwater at another stated hour
required the two hours to belong to the same system, or the timetable was a
work of fiction. Worse, the danger was not merely administrative. Single-track
working depended on knowing that the up train had cleared a section before the
down train entered it, and that knowledge was expressed in minutes. A signalman
whose watch disagreed with his neighbour's by four minutes was not making a
clerical error; he was arranging a collision. The Great Western adopted London
time across its whole route in 1840, and the other companies followed, so that
by the middle of the century a passenger stepping down onto a provincial
platform found two minute hands on the station clock: one for the railway, one
for the town.

The telegraph made the correction cheap and then made it universal. An
electrical signal despatched from Greenwich could set a clock in Edinburgh
within a fraction of a second, and once the Observatory began sending a daily
time signal along the wires, the argument for local time lost its last
practical support. What remained was sentiment, and a certain amount of civic
pride. Oxford resisted for years. Its cathedral bell was still tolling five
minutes after the hour, in obedience to the meridian of the city rather than
the meridian of the Observatory, long after the trains had stopped caring. The
law caught up slowly and without drama: the Statutes (Definition of Time) Act
of 1880 simply declared that any expression of time in an Act of Parliament
should mean Greenwich mean time in Great Britain, and the question was closed.

What is striking about the episode, looking back, is how little of it was
planned. No one set out to abolish local time. Engineers solved a scheduling
problem, telegraph companies sold a service, and a legal definition was
eventually written down to tidy up what had already happened. The standard
emerged from the constraints of the fastest thing in the system, and every
slower institution was reorganised around it afterwards. Precisely the same
pattern repeated a few years later on a larger scale, when the delegates at
Washington divided the world into hours of longitude, and again in our own
century, when the atomic definition of the second detached timekeeping from
the rotation of the earth altogether and left astronomers to insert leap
seconds by hand.

There is a general lesson buried here about the way infrastructure changes.
The visible reform, the one that gets a date and an Act, is usually the last
step rather than the first. It ratifies a settlement that the underlying
technology had already forced, and it is therefore easy to mistake the
ratification for the cause. Historians of the period who read the 1880 Act as
the moment Britain adopted a national time have the sequence exactly backwards.
The country had adopted it forty years earlier, one railway company at a time,
because a signalman needed to know what his neighbour's watch said. The
interesting question is never who wrote the standard down. It is which
constraint made the old arrangement impossible to keep.
