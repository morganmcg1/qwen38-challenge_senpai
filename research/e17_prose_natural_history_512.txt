We came to anchor in the lee of the island a little before noon, in seven
fathoms over clean sand, and I was set ashore upon a beach of grey shingle
that rattled underfoot like a shovelled hearth. The island is low at its
northern end and rises southward to a bluff of dark weathered rock, perhaps
ninety feet above the water, and its whole western face is scoured by a wind
that appears never wholly to cease. I had three hours before the boat was to
return for me, and I spent them chiefly upon the tide flat, which the falling
water had that morning laid bare to a breadth I judged at four hundred yards.

The flat was occupied by two waders which I take to be closely allied, though
they keep apart and feed after wholly different fashions. The larger, of which
I secured a single specimen, measured ten inches and a half from bill tip to
tail, the bill alone two inches and two lines, straight and stout, the tarsus
an inch and a half, the folded wing five inches and nine lines. Its plumage
was a sober ash above, freckled at the shoulders, the belly white, the legs a
dull olive. It fed in the shallowest wash of the ebb, walking with a slow
deliberate gait and probing vertically to the full depth of the bill, and I
watched one bird take eleven small worms from a space of ground no larger than
my hat. The smaller species, scarcely eight inches over all, with a bill of
one inch and one line and noticeably decurved at the tip, would not enter the
water at all, but ran along the wet margin in short bursts, snatching at what
the retreating film uncovered. Where the two came within a few feet of one
another the smaller invariably gave way.

Among the rocks at the foot of the bluff the ebb leaves a chain of pools, the
largest some fifteen feet across and not above two feet deep at the centre. I
found the water in it at seventy-one degrees, the open sea at the same hour
standing at sixty-three, so that its tenants endure within six hours a change
of temperature no creature of the deeper water is ever called upon to bear.
Anemones of two kinds were abundant, the one a translucent green with some
fifty short tentacles, contracting at a touch to a firm button no bigger than
a pea, the other larger and brick coloured and disposed always in the shaded
overhangs where the sun does not reach. Upon the walls were limpets in numbers
I could not estimate, each seated in a scar of its own grinding; I marked
several with a scratch of the knife, and two had returned to the same station
when I passed again two hours later.

The vegetation ascends the shore in bands so regular that a man might survey
them with a line. Nothing grows below the level of ordinary high water save
the weeds themselves. From that line to some eight feet above it there is a
fleshy prostrate herb with opposite glaucous leaves, full of water and
yielding a saline juice when crushed, which I judge the first colonist of
every crevice that will hold a spoonful of sand. Above it comes a coarse
tussock grass, at first in isolated hummocks and then continuously, and above
the grass a low shrub, much wind-pruned, dead and stripped on the exposed side
while the leeward side flowers freely. On the sheltered eastern face this
shrub attains four feet; on the western it is nowhere above fifteen inches,
though the root stocks are plainly of great age.

It is tempting to lay the whole arrangement to salt, but I believe the wind
the greater agent, and the two are not easily separated, since it is the wind
that carries the spray. As to the birds, I would gladly know whether the
smaller wader breeds here or merely touches the island in passing. I found no
nest, and the season is against it; yet the bird was in worn plumage and
showed no disposition to leave, while the larger species rose in a body toward
evening and went off northward with a purpose that suggested one stage of a
long journey. If the two are near relations, their difference of habit here is
instructive, being precisely what one would expect had a common stock been
divided by the depth of water each could profitably work. Neither is likely to
have reached so remote a station by its own strength alone; the prevailing set
of the current from the north-west, and the drift timber lodged high on that
shore, suggest by what road the plants at least arrived.
